Pocasset Water Quality Coalition Proposes Additional Regulations To Address Cesspools

By Helen Sievert

August 14, 2026
Click here to view original publication in the Enterprise

The Pocasset Water Quality Coalition (PWQC) presented additional proposals to amend local regulations to address the impact of cesspools at the Bourne Board of Health’s meeting on Wednesday , August 12 .

Operating as a nonprofit organization, PWQC was founded in 2019 with the mission “to restore the water quality of Hen Cove, Barlow’s Landing Harbor and the inlet of Wings Neck to healthy environmental levels,” its website states.

Highlighting Patuisset Beach, which was closed from June 19 to July 1 earlier this summer due to increased levels of fecal coliform bacteria, PWQC attributes the beach’s annual closures to the “cesspools close to the shore,” in a statement to the Board of Health and Health Department. “This has now happened three times in the last five years at the beginning of summer,” it noted.

Citing an illustration of a cesspool by the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), PWQC President Keith Barber highlighted that a feature of a cesspool is that it does not have a bottom.

“Everything goes into the cesspool, and then where does it go?” Barber asked. “On Cape Cod, it goes into our porous soil.”

To address this, Barber had advocated during public comment at the board’s meeting on June 10 for changing the amount of time that Bourne residents have to upgrade an identified cesspool to a septic system from two years to one year.

Cesspools are nonconforming systems that do not align with the existing regulations as stipulated in 310 CMR 15.000, which is Title 5 of the Massachusetts State Environmental Code. They are automatically considered a failed system.

The Massachusetts Department of Environmental Protection and local boards of health enforce 310 CMR 15.000, which outlines the standards for on-site sewage disposal systems.

Specifically, 310 CMR 15.305 outlines the stipulations surrounding deadlines for completion of upgrades: “If a system is failing to protect public health, safety, welfare or the environment as set forth in 310 CMR 15.303(1) or 15.304(1), the owner or operator shall upgrade the system within two years of discovery unless a shorter period of time is set by the Local Approving Authority or the Department based upon the existence of an imminent health hazard.”

The Town of Bourne follows the state regulation, a representative from the Health Department said in a telephone call with the Enterprise on June 17. If a cesspool is identified, the property owners would be notified by the Health Department to replace the cesspool.

“Municipalities are able to make the rules more than the state,” Barber said on Wednesday.

In addition to the aforementioned proposal to decrease the time a property owner would have to upgrade an identified cesspool from two years to one year, PWQC’s statement to the Town of Bourne also recommended additional proposals with the goal of mitigating the presence of cesspools in Bourne.

PWQC’s additional proposals were as follows: “Extensions for replacing cesspools are limited to one extension request per property and extensions should be capped at six months. Obtain software package to track pump-out data now. Declare two pump-outs within 12 months requires a septic inspection. Declare three pump-outs in 12 months an automatic fail.”

At Wednesday’s meeting, Barber also shared the history of Title 5, which was originally adopted in 1975.

An update to the regulation in 1995 added the requirement for “sellers to test and certify their systems before closing on real estate,” and implemented the state license inspector program to provide standardization, Barber said.

He noted that in 1995, cesspools were also designated as non-conforming systems in 1995 by the Massachusetts Department of Environmental Protection (MassDEP).

Barber explained that PWQC’s concern is that cesspools are still prevalent 31 years after regulations deem them non-conforming.

“Does that imply that most of these homes have been under the same ownership for greater than 30 years?” board chairman Robert D. Collett IV asked.

“Yes,” replied PWQC Secretary Deanna Kardoos, as she highlighted that homes, specifically in the Patuisset and Heights areas of Pocasset, are passed down through generations.

“It’s beautiful,” Kardoos said of the area, but noted that the continued presence of cesspools is the area’s “dirty little secret.”

In 1997, the commonwealth established a tax credit system for home repairs, Barber noted.

In 2023, MassDEP revised Title 5 regulations governing Natural Resource Nitrogen Sensitive Areas in towns across Cape Cod to meet estuarine water quality standards. The 15 municipalities comprising the Cape had until July 7, 2025, to file a notice of intent (NOI), which Bourne had done.

The new regulations had stated that watershed permits in Title 5 are optional, meaning that if a town had not applied for the permit, the regulations would have required homeowners residing in those watersheds to upgrade their existing septic systems to the best available nitrogen-reducing technology, within the next five years.

However, since the town of Bourne submitted an NOI, it suspended this aforementioned requirement that homeowners would have had to fulfill. The town now has until 2030 to file its watershed permits for its two watershed areas—Phinneys Harbor and Megansett Squeteague Harbor—with MassDEP, and have the department approve them. The Select Board has been working to apply for the watershed permits, having last discussed the matter at its meeting on July 21 .

Barber shared that PWQC’s research on decreasing the time residents would have to upgrade an identified cesspool to a system from two years to one year “first of all, it’s doable. Second of all, it’s extremely needed.” PWQC had contacted several engineering firms, many of whom had stated that they could replace the cesspool within the proposed timeline.

The presence of cesspools has “been a concern of mine for a long time,” Select Board chairman Robert D. Collett IV said. “I think it’s important that we address this in some meaningful way. But we have to be deliberate about things, and make sure that it’s practical and legal and everything else.”

“We just have to discuss amongst ourselves how we might be able to pursue an effort that aligns with your goals,” he said of the board’s next steps.

In response to Barber inquiring about a timeline for the proposal’s future, Collett replied the board will have to discuss how to potentially implement a new regulation: “It’s not something that we can do tonight or even at the next meeting.” He noted the need for additional input from the community, town counsel, Barnstable County, and MassDEP.

“It’s not something that can be done in a real short order, but I understand the sense of urgency and the need,” Collett stated.

Barber inquired how the PWQC may be able to expedite the process, to which Collett suggested that the organization could continue to provide information.

Collett added that the process may involve locating unidentified cesspools in Bourne, to which Barber said that PWQC members, half a dozen of whom were present at Wednesday’s meeting, would be willing to assist with.

Kardoos also reiterated PWQC’s proposal for the town to obtain a software package that could serve as a database to track the number of pump-outs a property has in a year. “If the system is required to be pumped out more than two times a year, you start right there–something’s wrong,” she said. She noted that some property owners may not even be aware that they have a cesspool since, due to the Cape’s sandy soil, a lot of the time the cesspool’s effluent “just keeps going down and washing down into the salt water.”

Acknowledging the cost of replacing a failed system, Barber noted that residents could contact Cape Cod AquiFund for financial assistance. He added that the commonwealth also offers a program that provides a 60% tax rebate for the cost of the project, capped at $18,000, with unused credit that can carry forward for up to five years.

In response to the presentation, board vice chairwoman Margaret Reali said she was “just taking in information.”

“It’s going to require a lot of conversation between other boards too, when you start to think about it, before you can make a final decision,” board member William “Dusty” Meier said of the process of discussing adopting the proposed regulation.

Appreciative of the PWQC’s efforts, Collett noted that the board is “aware of the problem” in regard to the cesspools and the proposal is “something we can at least start with.”

He reiterated, however, that adopting the proposed regulations “is a big thing, especially when you’re imposing huge expenses on people. It’s not something we can just do without a great deal of deliberation.”

“This isn’t just a Pocasset problem,” Barber said. “This is a Gray Gables problem, Bournedale problem, Sagamore Beach problem.” Collett agreed, noting it is “all across the town.”
