Advertising Firm Consulted Amid Efforts To Redesign Bourne's Town Seal

By Helen Sievert

August 14, 2026

The Bourne Historical Commission and Bourne Select Board/Herring Pond Wampanoag Tribe chairwoman Melissa A. Ferretti gained a better understanding of the process of redesigning the Town of Bourne’s seal after hearing a proposal from Pierce-Coté Advertising on Tuesday , August 11 .

Efforts to redesign Bourne’s town seal began after the Select Board voted to revise the town seal on February 21, 2024; however, the process was paused. Eventually, Ferretti, Historical Commission chairman Steven Philbrick and member Jack MacDonald resumed conversations on the matter.

At a Select Board meeting on June 30 , Philbrick shared that Pierce-Coté Advertising’s President Brad Schiff had accepted his invitation to attend the Historical Commission’s meeting in August. Ferretti had provided the firm’s contact information after receiving it from the Town of Mashpee, following the firm’s recent redesign of Mashpee’s town seal.

Schiff, alongside Pierce-Coté Advertising Senior Vice President Diane McPherson, presented a proposal for redesigning the Town of Bourne’s seal on Tuesday.

Schiff outlined the firm’s proposal and the several benchmarks of the collaborative project, all the way from the first meeting to presenting the final design.

A first major step would be to develop a structured and promoted town-wide survey to have residents “give their ideas,” Schiff said. It is “critical” to “get everybody’s feedback,” since the seal is “owned by the town,” he added. The firm and the town would also collaborate to determine logistics of disseminating the survey to Bourne residents.

A town seal committee would be established to work with the firm to facilitate discussions about the town seal. Members would be able to share their feedback and generate a mandate and dos and don’ts for the new town seal, Schiff said.

He noted that Mashpee’s town seal committee “brought different perspectives and it really worked well.” The committee had representatives from the Select Board, the town administrator, the town planner, the library director, the school superintendent, the Historical Commission and the Tribal Council.

In response to commission associate member Treather Gassman asking about having residents on the committee, Schiff recommended a “cross section of stakeholders.”

Compiling the information gathered from the brainstorming sessions of the town seal committee and the public’s responses to the town seal, the next step would be to develop and finalize a creative brief which would outline the objectives for the new seal.

He said that a value proposition will be developed that will assist in determining the seal’s imagery and tone. It will ask “what makes Bourne so distinct,” Schiff said, noting that the proposition is the “basis for developing the seal.”

“You want to have something that is going to represent, probably the past, and also looking into the future,” Schiff said of redesigning the new seal.

The next phase would feature designing the seal. “That’s where the fun part starts,” Schiff said.

Taking into account all of the feedback and research, the firm would produce several drafts of the town seal, he explained. The town seal committee would then choose and finalize a design, which would then be presented before the Select Board for its approval. If approved, it would then go before Town Meeting.

Once all approvals have been granted, Schiff and his team would create a brand standard for the many applications of the town seal, for example on municipal vehicles, letterheads and in buildings. Noting that the town currently has various iterations of the seal, establishing a brand standard would ensure that there is just one iteration.

After concluding the outline of the project, Schiff shared that the project’s timeline is approximately four to five months. He proposed that within that time frame, the firm and the committee meet every two weeks to “keep it with a cadence to keep it rolling.”

In response to Ferretti’s inquiry about when the timeline would start regarding getting funds approved, Schiff said the timeline would begin once that is obtained.

The cost for the aforementioned services is $9,500. Schiff said that this budget is “based on the hours and people-power to get this done.”

Both Philbrick and Ferretti agreed that the proposed cost “isn’t too bad.”

Ferretti also asked if the budget included cost analysis, to which Schiff replied that the town would be responsible for the analysis for applying the new seal throughout town. McPherson suggested a five-year rollout for applying the new seal.

Prefacing that the proposal is “great,” Ferretti said the four-to-five-month time is “ambitious” and noted aligning the members’ various schedules.

“I don’t disagree with you,” Schiff said. “Let’s put it this way. We could get it done in that time period, but it is the reality of people’s availability.” He proposed that the town seal committee and the firm would meet to finalize a timeline that feels “comfortable.”

Ferretti emphasized that she wants to make sure that the process is “done very respectfully and not rushed on our residents” and avoids creating tension or problems.

“We want it to be something everybody wants to do, not something that people resent,” she added. Schiff agreed.

Acknowledging the current seal’s history as it was drawn by local artist Charles S. Raleigh, Ferretti stated the importance of properly delivering and timing the redesign of the town seal and getting all residents on board. Raleigh had been born in England in the early 1800s and eventually settled in Bourne, dying there in 1925.

She also noted that the new seal should unify the Town of Bourne and the Herring Pond Wampanoag Tribe.

Since the imagery should unite and encapsulate the entire town, Mahoney proposed it should not depict just a specific village in town or symbols that physically divide the town.

McPherson emphasized the importance of getting “buy-in on investment of use” of the redesigned town seal and that Bourne residents feel a sense of ownership over the seal.

“There is more to discuss than just the design,” Ferretti noted about the process.

Noting that it is an “ongoing process,” Philbrick proposed that the first step be creating a town seal committee. Ferretti said that the committee would have to be appointed by the Select Board.

Residents embracing the process of redesigning the town seal and the final product is significant, Ferretti said. Reiterating that she does not want the process to create tension but rather be respectful instead, she explained that the new seal would fix historical inaccuracies that are featured on the current seal.

Commission member Jack MacDonald agreed that the redesign is about respecting culture. Commission secretary Deborah M. Burgess highlighted the several historical inaccuracies on the current seal, such as its inaccurate depiction of boats in the background and the Tribe’s attire and housing.

Burgess also suggested several aspects of Bourne’s history that the new seal could highlight, such as the Aptuxcet Trading Post. Commission vice chairman Donald J. Mahoney agreed, noting that it is the oldest enterprise in the region, as it was built in 1627.

Bourne’s history of being part of the Town of Sandwich until it was separately incorporated in 1884 could also be included in the seal, Burgess suggested. She also proposed that the interior portion of the seal have “Bourne” written on it.

Her proposal to include corn and Wampum beads was well received by Ferretti.

Mahoney noted that this process could also catalyze getting Bourne residents interested in the history of their town.

Adding onto Ferretti’s suggestion that Bourne’s schools could also get involved, Gassman proposed that it could be featured in the school system’s curriculum.

The firm would design what the town seal committee ultimately agrees on for the final design after receiving public feedback and its own brainstorming session, Schiff said.

Ferretti said she would talk with Town Administrator Marlene V. McCollem about the matter during their weekly discussions and get it on the Select Board’s agenda.
